# Writing for the blog

Horatio has a blog of its own: articles on playing and running a game, for everyone who uses Horatio, not one troupe. The staff of any troupe that isn't archived write for it, narrators included, and the custodian reads every article before it goes up. Readers can't comment.

This page is the writing guide: what suits the blog, the rules on quoting the books and on pictures, and how an article gets from your draft to the blog.

## Where to write

Choose your name at the top of any page, and then **Write for the blog**, beside **See your profile**. On a phone or a narrow window, it is also in the menu, under your name. On a troupe's **Overview**, **Write for the blog** at the top of **Waiting on you** starts an article that names that troupe.

**Write for the blog** lists **Your articles**, the latest changed first. Each one says where it stands, such as **Draft**, **Waiting for the custodian**, **Returned to you** or **Published**, with its dates and any note from the custodian. Choose an article to open it, or **Write an article** to start one.

## What suits the blog

Write about how to play and how to run a game, from your own experience: what worked at your table, what didn't, and what you'd tell someone starting out. For example:

- running your first game, or your first Elysium;
- storytelling, scenes and plots that pull players in;
- safety and consent at the table, and looking after new players;
- tips for players: building a character, roleplaying a clan, downtimes;
- running a troupe: staff, house rules, recruiting, keeping records in Horatio.

Write for people who don't know your troupe. Name characters and events only where it helps, and never write about a real person in a way they wouldn't want: a player's lines and veils, what happened between players, or anyone's private details. If you wouldn't say it at a public convention panel, leave it out.

The blog is public: anyone can read a published article, signed in or not. Its reading pages aren't open yet: until they are, a published article is seen only by its author and the custodian.

## Quoting the books

**Never paste text from the books into an article.** The rulebooks belong to their publishers, and the blog is public. Explain a rule in your own words instead, and give the book and page so readers can look it up, such as "MET: VTM, p. 295" for blood bonds.

A short quote is fine where the exact words matter: a sentence or two, in quotation marks, saying where it is from. Credit anyone else you quote, and link to where you found it.

## Pictures

An article can have one picture across its top. Use only a picture you made yourself or have the right to share. Never scan or photograph art from the books, and don't use a photo of someone without asking them.

Save the article as a draft first, then choose **Add a picture** under **Picture**. A JPEG, PNG, WebP or HEIC picture up to 10 MB works. Horatio asks you to place a frame twice as wide as it is high: drag it, or use the arrow keys, and size it with **Size**. What is outside the frame is cut off. Choose **Save the picture**. **Change the picture** places a new one, and **Remove the picture** takes it off. Where a photo was taken and other details phones add are removed.

## Writing an article

1. Open **Write for the blog** and choose **Write an article**.
2. Give it a **Title** and a **Summary**: a line or two, shown under the title and wherever the article is listed.
3. Under **Troupe**, choose the troupe you're on the staff of that the article names, or **None**. If you're on the staff of several, choose one. Readers can follow its name to the troupe's public profile. A troupe whose profile isn't public, or that is archived, shows its name only, with no link.
4. Write the article in the editor, with headings, lists, quotes and links from its buttons.
5. Under **Tags**, choose what it's about from the custodian's list, such as **Storytelling** or **Safety and consent**. The custodian may change them before it goes up.
6. Choose **Save draft**. Only you and the custodian can see a draft.

At the top, a line says where the article stands and what happens next. Each article also has an address on the blog, made from its title, such as /blog/running-your-first-elysium.

### Preview

Choose **Preview** at the top of the article to see it as the blog will show it: its picture, tags, title, summary, your name and troupe, the words, and under them the card about you from **Account › Blog author**. If you haven't set that up yet, **Set up your author profile** takes you there; the list of your articles has the same link. A banner says it is a preview: nobody else sees it until the custodian publishes it. Choose **Write** to carry on.

## How approval works

1. When it's ready, choose **Submit to the custodian**. It saves what you've written and sends it. While it waits, you can't change it: choose **Withdraw** to take it back and make changes, then submit it again.
2. The custodian reads it, and either publishes it or returns it to you with a note. You're told on your bell either way.
3. If it comes back, it's a draft again, and the note is at the top of the article and in **Your articles**. Change what the note asks and submit it again.
4. Once it's published, anyone can read it on the blog.

### Changing a published article

You can still change a published article. Edit it as before, then choose **Propose the changes**. Readers keep seeing the published version until the custodian publishes your changes; if they return them, the published version stays as it is, and their note tells you why. **Save the changes** keeps them without sending them, **Withdraw the changes** takes back changes that are waiting, and **Throw away the changes** drops them, leaving the article as it was published.

Its address stays the same once it has been published, even if you change the title, so links to it keep working.

### Unpublished articles

The custodian can take a published article down, with a reason you're told on your bell and see at the top of the article. You can change it and submit it again; once published, it is back at the same address.

### Deleting a draft

**Delete the draft** removes an article that has never been published, as long as it isn't waiting for the custodian. It can't be brought back. A published article stays: only the custodian can take it down.
